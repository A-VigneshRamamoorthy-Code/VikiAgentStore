# Loops, escalation and endings

The hook buys ten seconds. Structure buys the rest.

---

## The four-stage loop

1. **Open** — plant a legible question. Not "something incredible happened", but
   "why did the bell ring thirteen times?"
2. **Progress** — supply a clue, an attempt, or a narrowed possibility.
3. **Complicate** — let partial resolution create a harder question.
4. **Pay** — answer clearly, and show the consequence.

**Loop types:** outcome (will she?), cause (why did it?), method (how?),
character (will he admit it?), moral (was it worth it?), clock (what happens at
zero?).

---

## Stacking and nesting

- **Stacked** — several unresolved questions running in parallel.
- **Nested** — one story formally encloses another:
  `A opens → B opens → C opens → C closes → B closes → A closes`.
  Close in **reverse order of opening**.

**Rules that hold in short form:**

- Keep **at most three live loops**. A fourth reads as confusion, not intrigue.
- Touch every live loop every **8–20 seconds** with genuine progress — a reminder
  is not progress.
- Do not close everything at the midpoint. If every question is answered, the
  remaining runtime is denouement and viewers leave.
- Do not defer everything to the end either. Pay micro-loops throughout while one
  governing uncertainty survives.

**A clean handoff** closes one loop and worsens another in two sentences:

> "The thirteenth strike was a flood warning."  ← C closes
> "But the mechanism had been removed eighty years ago."  ← A and B worsen

## The loop ledger

Draw this before writing. It is the single most useful artefact in the skill.

```text
A — governing question   OPEN 0:06  PROGRESS 1:11         CLOSE 2:02
B — immediate goal       OPEN 0:28  PROGRESS 0:43, 0:52   CLOSE 1:41
C — clue / mini-mystery  OPEN 0:52                        CLOSE 1:16
D — ticking clock        OPEN 0:58                        CLOSE 2:07
```

If a loop has no `CLOSE`, either write one or cut the loop. There is no third
option.

## Re-hooks

A **re-hook** gives a fresh story reason to keep watching: changed odds, new
evidence, a choice, or a consequence. A **pattern interrupt** only changes the
rhythm. A short sentence is a pattern interrupt; a short sentence carrying a
reversal is a re-hook.

**Placement:** ~25%, ~50%, ~70–80% of runtime. Treat as scaffolding, not law —
then audit every one to three sentences for real progress.

**On intervals:** the authenticated MrBeast production manual describes major
re-engagements at ~3 and ~6 minutes in videos averaging 13:37 — about 22% and
44% of runtime. Claims like "re-hook every 5–10 seconds" are **[FOLKLORE]**;
they are unsourced and conflate cuts with story turns.

| Device | What the narrator does | Example | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loop callback | Reprises the unpaid promise *with* progress | "And the letter was still unopened." | After a detour |
| Deadline update | Turns elapsed time into pressure | "Sixty seconds had become eighteen." | After a failed attempt |
| Tone shift | Explanatory → intimate | "That is the official version. He remembered it differently." | Before a personal cost |
| Short sentence | Breaks a longer run | "The train didn't." | At a reversal |
| One-word sentence | Isolates the discovery | "Thirteen." | Once, at the strongest beat |
| Direct address | Puts the viewer in the decision | "You have the same two options she had." | Before a dilemma |
| Rhetorical question | Names the next gap | "So why was the safest lever locked?" | At a section turn |
| Contradiction | Replaces the viewer's model | "Except it was not a warning. It was permission." | Midpoint or reveal |
| New character | Introduces agency | "Then a second bidder raised his hand." | When conflict stabilises |
| Time jump | Compresses routine | "Three winters passed." | After causality is established |
| Admission | Resets trust | "Here is the part I got wrong." | Before corrected evidence |
| Stat drop | Widens scale, then interprets | "Two metres in eleven minutes — faster than anyone could run." | After a human-scale scene |
| False victory | Relief, then its price | "The signal turned red. The train accelerated." | ~45–65% |
| Rule change | Alters what winning means | "Stopping it was no longer possible. Choosing where was." | Before the final attempt |

## Escalation

**Causality:** join every beat with **but** or **therefore**. If the joint is
*and then*, the beats are merely adjacent and the middle will sag.

**Try-fail cycles.** Two or three before the decisive action:

- **"No, and…"** — the attempt fails, *and* conditions worsen.
- **"Yes, but…"** — the attempt succeeds, *but* at a cost that raises the stakes.

Escalate by **removing options** and raising cost, urgency, intimacy, or
irreversibility. A protagonist with three ways out is not in trouble.

**Make the viewer care in twenty seconds:** give the character a capability, a
relationship, a want, and — decisively — an *active choice*. Passive characters
do not hold attention regardless of how sympathetic they are.

**The slow beat.** Place one deliberate quiet beat before the climax. Without
contrast the fast beats do not read as fast. This is the most commonly skipped
step and the most obvious in its absence.

## Signposting without spoiling

Reveal the **danger, destination or category of outcome**. Conceal the **route,
choice, cost or identity**.

| | |
|---|---|
| Too vague | "And then everything changed." |
| Spoils it | "She diverted the train and saved her son." |
| Productive | "In ninety seconds, either the train or the parade would reach the crossing." |
| Outcome-known suspense | "She saved the parade. What it cost her is the story." |

## Endings

Four jobs, in order:

1. **Resolve the action** — what happened.
2. **Pay the opening promise** — why or how.
3. **Show what changed** — the emotional meaning.
4. **Land one final image** — not a lecture.

**An earned reveal requires** a clue planted beforehand, a plausible alternative
reading, a causal link to prior action, changed understanding rather than hidden
trivia, and a consequence after disclosure.

**Loop seams** — how the ending invites a rewatch:

- **Echo** — repeat the opening line with changed meaning.
- **Causal** — the ending creates the event seen at the start.
- **Reinterpretation** — the last fact makes the opening worth replaying.
- **Adjacent question** — close the promise, leave one legitimate aftershock.

The seam must never withhold the answer that was promised. "Part two for the
explanation" is unpaid debt.

**Cheap endings:** coincidence rescues the protagonist · the solution uses
something never planted · it was a dream · the reveal contradicts rather than
reinterprets · the protagonist goes passive at the decisive moment · shock
without consequence · the hook promised one answer and the ending gives another ·
it ends on "like and subscribe" instead of its button.

## Share and comment triggers

Written into the narration, not appended to it:

| Trigger | How | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate controversy | Stage two defensible values | Inverted-U: extreme controversy suppresses sharing |
| Open interpretation | Close the facts, leave the motive debatable | Never leave the promised fact unpaid |
| Bounded invitation | Ask about the exact choice just witnessed | Beats a generic "what do you think?" |
| Relatability | Name a precise private habit | Specific recognition beats "we've all been there" |
| Identity signal | Frame a value distinction viewers can wear | Give both sides dignity |
| Practical value | Embed a portable sentence | Must emerge from events, not interrupt them |

Before adding a prompt, ask: does sharing this make the sender look perceptive,
caring or useful? Does the meaning survive being retold in one sentence? If not,
"share this" will not manufacture it.

## Beat map — a 60–120 second narrated story

Assumes ~150 wpm; scale words to your chosen pace.

| 60s | 120s | Words 60/120 | Job | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | 0:00–0:12 | 20 / 30 | Hook into a concrete character and anomaly | Open **A** |
| 0:08–0:16 | 0:12–0:25 | 20 / 32 | Capability, want, first active choice | Open **B** |
| 0:16–0:26 | 0:25–0:43 | 25 / 45 | Try-fail 1 — *No, and*. Re-hook | Progress A/B |
| 0:26–0:36 | 0:43–1:03 | 25 / 50 | Try-fail 2 — *Yes, but*. New rule or clue | Open **C** |
| 0:36–0:43 | 1:03–1:14 | 18 / 28 | **Slow beat.** Let the loss register | Open nothing |
| 0:43–0:51 | 1:14–1:36 | 20 / 55 | Reversal; C's answer creates the final dilemma | Close **C**, open **D** |
| 0:51–0:57 | 1:36–1:52 | 15 / 40 | Final choice and action | Close **D**, **B**, **A** |
| 0:57–1:00 | 1:52–2:00 | 7 / 20 | Emotional button, loop seam | Nothing new |

If exposition overruns its budget, cut names and backstory before cutting the
attempts or the payoff.

## Long form — 8 to 20 minutes

The four-stage loop does not scale by stretching. At ten minutes a single
promise/progress/payoff arc goes slack in the middle, and a viewer who looks
away at 4:00 has no way back in. Nest the loops inside **acts**, and make the
acts visible.

### Three acts, each ending on an elevation

| Act | Share of runtime | Job | Ends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | ~25% | Establish the world and the ordinary; start the clock | The moment the ordinary breaks |
| II | ~50% | Escalation. Two or three try-fail cycles, each raising the cost | The lowest point *turned* — a decision, not a defeat |
| III | ~25% | Consequence and meaning | The image that answers the opening image |

**An act must end on a high, not a stop.** The end of an act is the one place a
viewer gives themselves permission to leave, so it has to arrive as a turn —
a reversal, a revelation, a decision taken. If an act simply runs out of
material and fades, you have a chapter boundary, not an act break.

### Chapter cards are load-bearing

Name each act on screen. In a long piece this is not decoration: it is the
mechanism that converts "this is all over the place" into "I know where I am".
It also gives a returning viewer a re-entry point, and gives you a legitimate
place to reset pace after a heavy beat.

### Re-hook on a fixed cadence

Open or advance a loop roughly every **90 seconds**. In practice that means
every act carries its own small promise, and no stretch of narration longer
than about a minute and a half is pure exposition.

### Follow the sequence

When the subject is a real chronology, tell it in order. Cross-cutting between
locations is what makes a factual piece feel scattered — unless every switch
is anchored the instant it happens, with a place (a pin on a map the viewer has
already learned) and a time (a marker on a timeline they have already seen).
Anchored, cross-cutting builds dread; unanchored, it reads as disorganisation.

### Land the ending

Long form ends badly far more often than it starts badly. Ending on the last
*fact* is what makes a film feel like it stops rather than finishes. Budget
three separate beats after the climax:

1. **Consequence** — what changed because of this.
2. **Meaning** — one line of interpretation, and only one.
3. **The button** — an image or phrase that rhymes with the opening.

Then let provenance have its own beat in the tail, where nothing competes with
it. Credits scattered through the film as background furniture are neither read
nor legible; a source card at the end is both.
