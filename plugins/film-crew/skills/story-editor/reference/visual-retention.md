# Visual retention

The narration decides whether the story holds. The picture decides whether the
eye stays on it.

---

## Cut rhythm

| Genre | Avg shot length | Cuts/min |
|---|---|---|
| Short-form viral | 1.0–1.7s | 35–60+ |
| Retention entertainment | 1.4–2.0s | 30–45 |
| Video essay | 2.5–4.0s | 15–25 |
| Premium documentary | 4.0–6.0s | 10–15 |

**Cut on the exhaustion of information, not on a timer.** A shot ends when the
eye has finished reading it. Holding past that point bleeds viewers; cutting
before it feels frantic and unearned.

**[FOLKLORE]** "Cut every three seconds or they swipe." — and its cousin,
"something must change every five seconds." Both conflate the shot change with
the narrative turn. Fast cutting over a static story is a music video, not
retention. Cut often *and* move the story; they are two different jobs, and only
the second one keeps anybody watching.

**The cut toolbox**

- **Energy cut / jump cut** — removes breath and dead space, accelerating cadence.
- **J-cut** — the *next* shot's audio starts before the picture changes. The most
  useful retention cut there is: the ear hooks first, so the eye waits.
- **L-cut** — picture changes while the previous audio continues. Good for
  reactions and b-roll without breaking flow.
- **Match cut** — shape or motion continues across a scene change; preserves
  eye-trace perfectly.
- **Smash cut** — abrupt calm→chaos. Reclaims wandering attention through contrast.

Aim to overlap roughly 80% of transitions with a J- or L-cut. Hard cuts on both
picture and sound simultaneously feel like a slideshow.

## Pattern interrupts

Use **2–3 per minute maximum**. They work by violating an expectation; used
constantly they *become* the expectation.

| Device | Effect | Use sparingly because |
|---|---|---|
| Scale change (wide→macro) | Resets spatial attention | Overuse disorients |
| Speed ramp | Kinetic energy on a peak moment | Reads as dated travel-vlog |
| Colour shift | Marks a change of time or mood | Breaks visual cohesion without cause |
| Freeze frame | Halts momentum for commentary | Momentum must be rebuilt from zero |
| Zoom punch-in | Emphasises a realisation | Arbitrary unless motivated by tone |
| Aspect-ratio change | Signals archival or cinematic | Can look broken if not clearly stylised |
| Dip to black | Creates a vacuum before a reveal | Over ~1.5s on short-form triggers a swipe |
| Medium shift (live→animation) | Changes the rules of the world | Expensive; clashing styles disjoint the story |

## Framing

- **Centre framing** wins when the subject *is* the information — direct address,
  short-form A-roll. It maximises parasocial eye contact.
- **Rule of thirds** when the environment carries meaning, or text shares the frame.
- **Depth and foreground occlusion** — something out of focus between camera and
  subject creates parallax on any move, which holds the eye far longer than a
  flat subject against a wall.
- **Eye-trace** — if the subject sits top-left in shot A, the important element in
  shot B should be near top-left too. Making the eye hunt after a cut costs
  attention.

**Shot sizes carry meaning:** wide = "here is the world" · medium = "here is the
conversation" · close = "here is the emotion" · extreme close = "here is the
truth".

## Motion

Nothing in nature moves linearly, which is why linear motion looks cheap. Ease
everything.

| Move | Parameter |
|---|---|
| Continuous push-in | **1.5–3% per second** |
| Ken Burns on a still | **4–6% over 4s** plus slow positional drift |
| Text pop-in | **4–6 frames** (15 frames feels sluggish) |
| Synthetic camera shake | 0.5–1.5 Hz, 5–15 px amplitude |
| Easing | ~80% ease-out / 20% ease-in for graphics |

### Pace comes from beats, not from camera speed

"Make it faster" almost never means "move the camera faster". Move the camera
faster and you get lurching; the viewer reads it as a mistake, not as energy.
Pace is **how often something changes**, and the cheapest change is content:
an element entering, a figure landing, a highlight jumping to the next pin, a
figure being struck through.

Keep the camera slow and deliberate underneath all of it. A useful ceiling for
a travelling frame is **~0.35 screen widths per second at its peak** — note
*peak*, because a cubic ease runs three times its own average at the midpoint,
so a move budgeted on average speed is three times too fast exactly where the
eye is most sensitive to it.

Two corollaries worth internalising:

- **Never move the camera when nothing has changed.** A drift across static
  content has no motivation and is read as restlessness. Move to *greet* new
  content, then stop.
- **A held image still needs life.** When narration dwells on one picture for
  more than a few seconds, give the image itself a slow continuous sway rather
  than panning the camera across it. The frame stays still, the subject
  breathes, and nothing lurches.

## Captions

Captions raise watch time through dual coding — the viewer reads and hears the
same content. Word-by-word "karaoke" highlighting acts as a visual metronome.

- **3–5 words** on screen at once.
- Ultra-bold sans-serif; heavy drop shadow or a solid pill. Plain white text
  disappears against sky, snow and highlights.
- Highlight on the exact cadence of speech.

**Safe areas (1080×1920):** avoid the top 15%, the bottom 25%, and the right 15%
— platform UI sits there. Keep essentials inside the centre **1080×1000** box.

**The duplication rule:** *captions* duplicate the narration exactly; *graphics*
must not. If the voice says "the market crashed", the graphic says "DOWN 40%".
A graphic that repeats the words wastes the channel.

---

## Colour

Platforms compress shadows and highlights aggressively, and most viewing happens
on a dim phone. Grade for **mid-tone contrast** and keep primaries saturated; a
muddy cinematic grade dies on the target device.

Pick 2–3 colours and hold them. A palette shift is a legitimate chapter marker —
vivid present, desaturated past.

## Thumbnail and opening frame

- High-intensity emotion, strong subject/background separation, **3–4 words
  maximum**, and a visible curiosity loop.
- The thumbnail is the hook; the title is the context. They should not say the
  same thing.
- **The first frame must fulfil the thumbnail.** Any bait-and-switch is punished
  within the first second.

## B-roll for abstract narration

"Show the noun" fails on abstractions and produces stock-footage soup. Visualise
the *feeling*, not the dictionary definition.

| Narration | Literal (weak) | Metaphorical (strong) |
|---|---|---|
| "The brain is lazy." | A brain diagram | Water finding the path of least resistance down glass |
| "Inflation ate their savings." | Stacks of banknotes | A note dissolving at the edges inside a wallet |
| "An algorithmic echo chamber." | Someone scrolling | Pulling back through a tunnel of identical mirrors |
| "Built on a lie." | An office building | A block pulled from the base of a glowing tower |
| "He connected the dots." | A whiteboard | Constellations linking into a blueprint |
| "Too complex to manage." | Blinking servers | Gridlocked traffic resolving into a knot of red lines |

Beware the opposite failure: a visual so interesting it competes with the
sentence. During a dense line, the picture should be *simple*.

## Picture first, text second

A typed line is the least efficient way to deliver a concrete noun. If the
thing can be drawn, **draw it** — a car, a police officer, a boat, a burning
window, a crowd. Reserve on-screen text for what a picture genuinely cannot
carry: a number, a name, a verbatim quote, a date.

A film in which every beat is a caption is a slideshow of its own script. It
reads as competent and lands as nothing, because the viewer is reading rather
than watching, and reading is the mode they came here to avoid.

| Narration mentions | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| A place | `chip("COLABA")` | A pin on a map they have now seen five times |
| A time | `chip("21:44")` | A mark advancing down a timeline they know |
| A vehicle, a weapon, a person | The word | The drawing |
| A journey between places | "they moved north" | A thread drawn from pin to pin |
| A quantity | A sentence | The figure, alone, large |

Keep the vocabulary **small and repeated**. Five drawings a viewer learns beat
twenty they meet once; the recognition is what lets a later beat land without
explanation. The same discipline applies to the map: reuse one map and move the
highlight, rather than issuing a new picture each time.

## A visual beat plan

| Time | Move |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:02 | Hook. Extreme close-up, high internal motion. Matches thumbnail. |
| 0:02–0:05 | Context. Fast cuts, establishing wide, text pops in. |
| 0:05–0:15 | Setup. Medium shots, J-cuts between sentences, 2%/s push-in. |
| ~0:15 | **Interrupt 1** — punch-in or aspect change on the first hard fact. |
| 0:16–0:35 | Escalation. Metaphorical b-roll, 3–4s shots with internal motion. |
| ~0:35 | **Interrupt 2** — dip to black, half a second, the breath. |
| 0:36–0:50 | Climax. Fastest cutting, highest contrast, captions centred. |
| 0:50–end | Resolution. Slow to 4s shots, L-cuts, let the last line land alone. |
