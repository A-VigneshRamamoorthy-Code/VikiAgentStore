# Loops, escalation and endings

The hook buys ten seconds. Structure buys the rest.

---

## The four-stage loop

1. **Open** — plant a legible question. Not "something incredible happened", but
   "why did the bell ring thirteen times?"
2. **Progress** — supply a clue, an attempt, or a narrowed possibility.
3. **Complicate** — let partial resolution create a harder question.
4. **Pay** — answer clearly, and show the consequence.

**Loop types:** outcome (will she?), cause (why did it?), method (how?),
character (will he admit it?), moral (was it worth it?), clock (what happens at
zero?).

---

## Stacking and nesting

- **Stacked** — several unresolved questions running in parallel.
- **Nested** — one story formally encloses another:
  `A opens → B opens → C opens → C closes → B closes → A closes`.
  Close in **reverse order of opening**.

**Rules that hold in short form:**

`[HOUSE HEURISTIC]` — the three numbers below are working defaults that keep a
draft honest, not measured constants. No controlled study establishes any of
them. Use them to catch a script that has lost track of its promises; abandon
them the moment the material argues back.

- Keep **at most three live loops**. A fourth reads as confusion, not intrigue.
- Touch every live loop every **8–20 seconds** with genuine progress — a reminder
  is not progress.
- Do not close everything at the midpoint. If every question is answered, the
  remaining runtime is denouement and viewers leave.
- Do not defer everything to the end either. Pay micro-loops throughout while one
  governing uncertainty survives.

**What actually counts as progress.** This one is not negotiable, because it is
the difference between structure and the appearance of structure. A beat is
progress only if it changes at least one of:

**goal · odds · knowledge · cost · available options · relationship · time
remaining**

A callback that restates the question is not progress. A cut is not progress. A
zoom, a sound effect and a music swell are not progress. If you cannot name
which of those seven moved, the beat is decoration and the middle will sag
regardless of how it is shot.

**A clean handoff** closes one loop and worsens another in two sentences:

> "The thirteenth strike was a flood warning."  ← C closes
> "But the mechanism had been removed eighty years ago."  ← A and B worsen

## The loop ledger

Draw this before writing. It is the single most useful artefact in the skill.

```text
A — governing question   OPEN 0:06  PROGRESS 1:11         CLOSE 2:02
B — immediate goal       OPEN 0:28  PROGRESS 0:43, 0:52   CLOSE 1:41
C — clue / mini-mystery  OPEN 0:52                        CLOSE 1:16
D — ticking clock        OPEN 0:58                        CLOSE 2:07
```

If a loop has no `CLOSE`, either write one or cut the loop. There is no third
option.

## Re-hooks

A **re-hook** gives a fresh story reason to keep watching: changed odds, new
evidence, a choice, or a consequence. A **pattern interrupt** only changes the
rhythm. A short sentence is a pattern interrupt; a short sentence carrying a
reversal is a re-hook.

**Placement:** ~25%, ~50%, ~70–80% of runtime. `[HOUSE HEURISTIC]` — scaffolding
only, and weaker than it looks. Audit every one to three sentences for real
progress instead; the percentages exist to catch a script with none.

**On intervals — what the MrBeast handbook actually says.** The source is *How
to Succeed in MrBeast Production*, a **staff-verified circulated internal
document**, not an authenticated public rulebook: Business Insider confirmed it
with two former staffers but could not date it, and the document itself says
"This is not a rulebook" and asks to be read "with a grain of salt." It
describes major re-engagements at roughly three and six minutes in videos
averaging **818 seconds (13:37)** — about 22% and 44% of runtime.

Those are checkpoints for a *specific format*. Scoped honestly:

| Checkpoint | What it is for, in a 10–15 min challenge or spectacle video | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|
| ~1 minute | Promotion ends. "Stop telling people what they will be watching and start showing them" — the first attempt, demonstration or decision is visible | That every genre must show action by 60 s |
| ~20–25% | A simple, legible re-engagement — spectacle, or a differentiating moment | That every genre needs a spectacle here |
| ~40–45% | A complication or rule change that launches the back half | A universal six-minute timer |
| Back half | Denser explanation, now that the viewer is invested | Permission for the back half to be weaker |

**Claims to reject outright:**

- "Re-hook every 5–10 seconds" — `[FOLKLORE]`, unsourced, and conflates cuts
  with story turns.
- "Re-hook every 90 seconds" — `[HOUSE HEURISTIC]` at best; no evidence.
- "MrBeast resets the video every few minutes" — the phrase *reset the video*
  does not appear in the handbook or in verified interviews. The documented
  vocabulary is **re-engagement**, **spectacle**, **wow factor** and **stair
  stepping**.
- "MrBeast cuts every three seconds" — he cites this as an example of a *rigid
  editor rule that produces repetitive work*. On 3 March 2024 he publicly said
  he had slowed his videos down, focused on storytelling, let scenes breathe and
  made them longer, and advised creators to leave the ultra-fast-paced style
  behind. Whatever "MrBeast pacing" means, it is not a three-second timer.

**Stair stepping** is the technique worth taking. It means escalating one
variable the audience already understands: the firework video moves from a
one-dollar firework, through progressively more expensive ones, to a world
record. One axis, legible at every step, escalating.

**On the Zeigarnik effect.** It is the usual justification for open loops: that
an interrupted task is remembered better than a completed one. Treat that
version as **[FOLKLORE]** — a 2025 meta-analysis found no general memory
advantage for interrupted tasks.

The same meta-analysis did find support for the **Ovsiankina effect**: a general
tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks. That is nearer to what an open loop
does, and it is worth knowing the honest version exists — but it is laboratory
task resumption, and a viewer choosing to keep watching is not the same act.

So the load-bearing mechanism remains Loewenstein's information-gap theory:
curiosity is the discomfort of a gap between what you know and what you want to
know, relieved by closing the gap. That is also the more useful framing, because
it makes the open loop a *promise you owe the viewer* rather than a memory trick
you play on them — and a promise is something you can be held to.

| Device | What the narrator does | Example | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loop callback | Reprises the unpaid promise *with* progress | "And the letter was still unopened." | After a detour |
| Deadline update | Turns elapsed time into pressure | "Sixty seconds had become eighteen." | After a failed attempt |
| Tone shift | Explanatory → intimate | "That is the official version. He remembered it differently." | Before a personal cost |
| Short sentence | Breaks a longer run | "The train didn't." | At a reversal |
| One-word sentence | Isolates the discovery | "Thirteen." | Once, at the strongest beat |
| Direct address | Puts the viewer in the decision | "You have the same two options she had." | Before a dilemma |
| Rhetorical question | Names the next gap | "So why was the safest lever locked?" | At a section turn |
| Contradiction | Replaces the viewer's model | "Except it was not a warning. It was permission." | Midpoint or reveal |
| New character | Introduces agency | "Then a second bidder raised his hand." | When conflict stabilises |
| Time jump | Compresses routine | "Three winters passed." | After causality is established |
| Admission | Resets trust | "Here is the part I got wrong." | Before corrected evidence |
| Stat drop | Widens scale, then interprets | "Two metres in eleven minutes — faster than anyone could run." | After a human-scale scene |
| False victory | Relief, then its price | "The signal turned red. The train accelerated." | ~45–65% |
| Rule change | Alters what winning means | "Stopping it was no longer possible. Choosing where was." | Before the final attempt |

## Escalation

**Causality:** join every beat with **but** or **therefore**. If the joint is
*and then*, the beats are merely adjacent and the middle will sag.

**Name the axis before you write the cycles.** Escalation is not "more
exciting", it is one variable moving in one direction: **cost · scale · danger ·
rarity · intimacy · irreversibility · options remaining**. Write the axis down.
If attempt three is not harder, costlier, less reversible, more intimate or
narrower in options than attempt two, it is not an escalation — reorder it or
cut it. This is the same discipline as stair stepping, and it is what stops a
middle act becoming a list of things that happened.

**Try-fail cycles.** Two or three before the decisive action:

- **"No, and…"** — the attempt fails, *and* conditions worsen.
- **"Yes, but…"** — the attempt succeeds, *but* at a cost that raises the stakes.

Escalate by **removing options** and raising cost, urgency, intimacy, or
irreversibility. A protagonist with three ways out is not in trouble.

**Only real failures.** Never invent a setback to satisfy a beat map. Preserve a
genuine failure when it forces a new decision; if the material has no such
moment, write a different structure rather than staging one. Two independent
sources converge here: the MrBeast handbook's "We don't fake things" and "You
can't fake intensity", and Michelle Khare's account of building *Challenge
Accepted* around real fear and real failure — she studies *Survivor* and *Save
the Cat* and asks "What are the hills and the valleys? What is the all is
lost?", but the valley has to have actually happened.

For a factual film this is not merely an ethic. A staged setback creates a
payoff the footage cannot support, and the ledger will not have a claim to
reference when you reach it.

**Make the viewer care in twenty seconds:** give the character a capability, a
relationship, a want, and — decisively — an *active choice*. Passive characters
do not hold attention regardless of how sympathetic they are.

**On backstory.** The sources genuinely disagree: Donaldson treats participant
backstory as an unresolved tradeoff against pace, while character-led creators
build entire formats on vulnerability. The resolvable version: **keep backstory
that changes the present goal, stakes, relationship or choice; cut backstory
that only explains who someone is.**

**The slow beat.** Place one deliberate quiet beat before the climax. Without
contrast the fast beats do not read as fast. This is the most commonly skipped
step and the most obvious in its absence.

## Signposting without spoiling

Reveal the **danger, destination or category of outcome**. Conceal the **route,
choice, cost or identity**.

| | |
|---|---|
| Too vague | "And then everything changed." |
| Spoils it | "She diverted the train and saved her son." |
| Productive | "In ninety seconds, either the train or the parade would reach the crossing." |
| Outcome-known suspense | "She saved the parade. What it cost her is the story." |

## Endings

Four jobs, in order:

1. **Resolve the action** — what happened.
2. **Pay the opening promise** — why or how.
3. **Show what changed** — the emotional meaning.
4. **Land one final image** — not a lecture.

**An earned reveal requires** a clue planted beforehand, a plausible alternative
reading, a causal link to prior action, changed understanding rather than hidden
trivia, and a consequence after disclosure.

### The ending depends on the format

The sources disagree here, and the disagreement is real rather than resolvable
by picking a winner. The MrBeast handbook recommends a satisfying payoff
followed by an **abrupt** ending; documentary craft and character-led work
require consequence and meaning after the climax. Both are right about their own
form:

| Format | Ending |
|---|---|
| **Challenge / spectacle** | Payoff, immediate consequence, short button. Cut recap and housekeeping entirely |
| **Documentary / character** | Action, consequence, meaning, final image — the four beats below |
| **Short** | Pay the promise first. The replay seam is second, and optional |

What the handbook's "abrupt" does *not* mean is withholding the result. It means
removing the long signalled outro *after* the promised payoff has landed. Never
signal the ending in advance except to anticipate the promised payoff.

`[HOUSE HEURISTIC]` Never open a new loop that demands an answer in the final
10% of runtime.

**Loop seams** — how the ending invites a rewatch:

- **Echo** — repeat the opening line with changed meaning.
- **Causal** — the ending creates the event seen at the start.
- **Reinterpretation** — the last fact makes the opening worth replaying.
- **Adjacent question** — close the promise, leave one legitimate aftershock.

The seam must never withhold the answer that was promised. "Part two for the
explanation" is unpaid debt.

**On the claim that Shorts loops are algorithmically rewarded** — `[FOLKLORE]`.
Since March 2025 a replay does count as a public view, but YouTube's *engaged
views* metric explicitly **excludes loops**, and no loop-seam ranking bonus is
documented anywhere. Build a seam because a reinterpretation is satisfying, not
because you think it farms a metric. Pay the promise first, always.

**Cheap endings:** coincidence rescues the protagonist · the solution uses
something never planted · it was a dream · the reveal contradicts rather than
reinterprets · the protagonist goes passive at the decisive moment · shock
without consequence · the hook promised one answer and the ending gives another ·
it ends on "like and subscribe" instead of its button.

## Share and comment triggers

Written into the narration, not appended to it:

| Trigger | How | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate controversy | Stage two defensible values | Inverted-U: extreme controversy suppresses sharing |
| Open interpretation | Close the facts, leave the motive debatable | Never leave the promised fact unpaid |
| Bounded invitation | Ask about the exact choice just witnessed | Beats a generic "what do you think?" |
| Relatability | Name a precise private habit | Specific recognition beats "we've all been there" |
| Identity signal | Frame a value distinction viewers can wear | Give both sides dignity |
| Practical value | Embed a portable sentence | Must emerge from events, not interrupt them |

Before adding a prompt, ask: does sharing this make the sender look perceptive,
caring or useful? Does the meaning survive being retold in one sentence? If not,
"share this" will not manufacture it.

## Beat map — a 60–120 second narrated story

Assumes ~150 wpm; scale words to your chosen pace.

| 60s | 120s | Words 60/120 | Job | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | 0:00–0:12 | 20 / 30 | Hook into a concrete character and anomaly | Open **A** |
| 0:08–0:16 | 0:12–0:25 | 20 / 32 | Capability, want, first active choice | Open **B** |
| 0:16–0:26 | 0:25–0:43 | 25 / 45 | Try-fail 1 — *No, and*. Re-hook | Progress A/B |
| 0:26–0:36 | 0:43–1:03 | 25 / 50 | Try-fail 2 — *Yes, but*. New rule or clue | Open **C** |
| 0:36–0:43 | 1:03–1:14 | 18 / 28 | **Slow beat.** Let the loss register | Open nothing |
| 0:43–0:51 | 1:14–1:36 | 20 / 55 | Reversal; C's answer creates the final dilemma | Close **C**, open **D** |
| 0:51–0:57 | 1:36–1:52 | 15 / 40 | Final choice and action | Close **D**, **B**, **A** |
| 0:57–1:00 | 1:52–2:00 | 7 / 20 | Emotional button, loop seam | Nothing new |

If exposition overruns its budget, cut names and backstory before cutting the
attempts or the payoff.

## Long form — 8 to 20 minutes

The four-stage loop does not scale by stretching. At ten minutes a single
promise/progress/payoff arc goes slack in the middle, and a viewer who looks
away at 4:00 has no way back in. Nest the loops inside **acts**, and make the
acts visible.

### Three acts, each ending on an elevation

`[HOUSE HEURISTIC]` The 25/50/25 split is a convention inherited from screen
drama, not a measured optimum for video.

| Act | Share of runtime | Job | Ends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | ~25% | Establish the world and the ordinary; start the clock | The moment the ordinary breaks |
| II | ~50% | Escalation. Two or three try-fail cycles, each raising the cost | The lowest point *turned* — a decision, not a defeat |
| III | ~25% | Consequence and meaning | The image that answers the opening image |

**An act must end on a high, not a stop.** The end of an act is the one place a
viewer gives themselves permission to leave, so it has to arrive as a turn —
a reversal, a revelation, a decision taken. If an act simply runs out of
material and fades, you have a chapter boundary, not an act break.

### Chapter cards are load-bearing

Name each act on screen. In a long piece this is not decoration: it is the
mechanism that converts "this is all over the place" into "I know where I am".
It also gives a returning viewer a re-entry point, and gives you a legitimate
place to reset pace after a heavy beat.

**The one piece of evidence in the neighbourhood.** Guo, Kim and Rubin analysed
6.9 million viewing sessions across four edX courses and found median engagement
time capped at around six minutes regardless of how long the video ran
`[OBSERVATIONAL]`. Read it carefully: that is *instructional* video watched by
students, and it describes segmenting a lesson around one learning objective. It
is **not** a universal six-minute law for entertainment, and Mark Rober's
fifteen-to-thirty-minute films are a standing counterexample — he argues a
complete story cannot be told in one or two minutes.

What survives the translation is modest and worth keeping: **do not run a single
unbroken conceptual explanation for very long without a structural break.** A
chapter card, an act turn or a change of scene all qualify.

### Re-hook on a cadence

`[HOUSE HEURISTIC]` Open or advance a loop roughly every **90 seconds**. There
is no evidence for ninety specifically — it is a spacing that keeps exposition
from pooling. In practice it means every act carries its own small promise, and
no stretch of narration longer than about a minute and a half is pure
exposition. The obligation is *genuine progress at every section boundary*; the
interval is only how you notice you have stopped delivering it.

### Ask the viewer to predict

`[EXPERIMENT, transferred with care]` Inserting brief low-stakes questions into
a recorded lecture reduced mind-wandering and improved learning (Szpunar, Khan &
Schacter, PNAS 2013). The population was university students watching
twenty-one-minute statistics lectures, so the transfer to entertainment video is
an extrapolation and should be labelled as one.

The usable form is a **bounded invitation** — a question about the exact choice
just witnessed, placed before the answer arrives: *"You have the same two
options she had."* It re-recruits a wandering viewer by making them commit to a
prediction. Every few minutes at most; more often and it reads as a quiz.

Note this is the same device already listed as a re-hook, and the same
guardrail applies: ask about the specific decision on screen, never a generic
"what do you think?"

### Follow the sequence

When the subject is a real chronology, tell it in order. Cross-cutting between
locations is what makes a factual piece feel scattered — unless every switch
is anchored the instant it happens, with a place (a pin on a map the viewer has
already learned) and a time (a marker on a timeline they have already seen).
Anchored, cross-cutting builds dread; unanchored, it reads as disorganisation.

### Land the ending

Long form ends badly far more often than it starts badly. Ending on the last
*fact* is what makes a film feel like it stops rather than finishes. Budget
three separate beats after the climax:

1. **Consequence** — what changed because of this.
2. **Meaning** — one line of interpretation, and only one.
3. **The button** — an image or phrase that rhymes with the opening.

Then let provenance have its own beat in the tail, where nothing competes with
it. Credits scattered through the film as background furniture are neither read
nor legible; a source card at the end is both.

### Sponsor reads are a structural hole

An unrelated sponsor block gives the viewer a natural exit — it announces that
the story has stopped. Donaldson's stated preference is to make the sponsor's
funding or product *causally relevant* to the video, so the segment is part of
the events rather than an interruption of them `[CREATOR METHOD]`. The handbook
shows an internal retention graph with a larger drop on an old scripted read and
a smaller one on integrated brand material — an internal case comparison, not a
controlled trial.

The writable rule: **the sentence before the sponsor should create a need the
sponsor segment answers, and the sentence after it should continue the same
action.** Keep the advertising disclosure explicit regardless; an integration
that hides that it is an advertisement is a different problem.
