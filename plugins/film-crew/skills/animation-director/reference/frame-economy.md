# Frame economy

The numbers behind limited animation. Where a figure is industry consensus
repeated across secondary sources rather than something published by a studio,
it is marked *(consensus)* — treat those as sound working values, not as
citable fact.

## Ones, twos and threes

At 24fps, animation is timed by how many frames each drawing is held for.

| shorthand | drawings/sec | used for |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| on ones | 24 | fast action, debris, effects — rare on TV |
| on twos | 12 | the workhorse of theatrical animation |
| on threes | 8 | **the TV anime default** |

A 22-minute episode runs about 3,000–4,000 drawings *(consensus)*. Full
animation on ones would need upward of 12,000. The saving is not spread
evenly: dialogue and reaction shots drop to threes or to a single held cel,
and the surplus is concentrated in a handful of cuts.

Working on threes is not a downgrade. It changes the *texture* of movement —
motion reads as deliberate and posed rather than fluid — which is why the
style is legible as a choice rather than as a shortfall.

## Cuts per episode

A 22-minute episode contains roughly **250–350 cuts**, about 300 typically,
averaging **4.4 seconds** each *(consensus)*.

Scaled down, a **2-minute film wants roughly 26–30 cuts.** More than that and
no shot has time to register; fewer and the film stops feeling edited. The
validation story used 37 beats across 102 seconds — slightly dense, and it
showed: several beats had to be promoted out of `hold` simply to break up runs.

## How long a drawing may be held

| situation | limit |
|-----------|-------|
| static, camera parked | ≤ ~72 frames (3s) |
| static under a slow push | 2–8s, up to ~10s |
| impact freeze | 6–18 frames, never beyond 36 |

A held drawing with a moving camera can run three times longer than one
without, which is the whole reason the *yori* exists.

## The budget shape

Across an episode, the drawing budget is not uniform: the climax typically
takes **30–40%** of it. This is why an audit that flags "a long run of loud
shots" must exempt the final third — a sustained loud run at the climax is
correct, and the same run at the 20% mark is a pacing error.

## Where the drawings actually go

Two mechanisms recover most of the saving:

- **Eye blinks** — 3–4 frames every 90–120 frames. Cheap, and their absence is
  what makes a held face read as a photograph.
- **Kuchipaku lip sync** — exactly **three** mouth positions, held on twos,
  cycled without matching phonemes. Japanese TV animation does not attempt
  phoneme-accurate sync; the three-shape cycle is the convention, not a
  compromise.

Neither is expensive, and both are what keep a held cut from dying.
