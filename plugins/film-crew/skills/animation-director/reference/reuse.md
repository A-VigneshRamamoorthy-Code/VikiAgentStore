# Reuse

Drawing something once and spending it several times.

## Held backgrounds

The cheapest and oldest of these, and the one that pays twice.

A background is drawn once for a **scene** and held while the characters move
in front of it. It saves the expensive layer, and — the part that is easy to
miss — it is what makes the cheap layer *readable*. Motion means something only
against stillness. Once the ground stops changing, the eye reads whatever is
changing as the subject.

The corollary is a hard rule: **a held background must not be re-established.**
Fading the same hillside out and back in on every beat costs everything the hold
was buying and looks, on a motion profile, exactly like animation. On the
`style-paper` test film, re-establishing settings per beat drove loud and quiet
beats to within 1% of each other — the film measured as busy throughout and had
no contrast left to spend on the shots that mattered.

Scene boundaries are act boundaries. If a location changes mid-act, that is a
missing act break, not a background problem.

See [`style-paper/reference/art-direction.md`](../../style-paper/reference/art-direction.md)
for how this is implemented in the paper style.

## Walk and run cycles

| drawings | reads as |
|----------|----------|
| 8 | the standard cycle |
| 4 | acceptable, slightly stylised |
| 2 | the absolute floor — legible only in silhouette or at distance |

On twos, an 8-drawing cycle is a 16-frame stride.

The character is locked to the centre of frame and **the world moves past
them**. This is why the background must be at least 3× screen width: a
repeat inside the shot is the single most recognisable "cheap" tell.

A three-image loop is enough to carry a long walk if the camera is also
moving and something else — dust, a scarf, falling leaves — is drifting at a
different rate. The eye tracks the secondary motion and stops auditing the
cycle.

## Bank systems

A reusable library of shots: transformation sequences, establishing shots of a
recurring location, stock reaction cuts, title cards. Drawn once at high
quality, then reused for the run of the series.

The rule that makes banks work: **a bank shot must be tonally neutral or
match the scene it lands in.** Reusing a triumphant stock cut inside a defeat
is the failure people actually notice — far more than the reuse itself.

## Blue Lock — a worked example

*Blue Lock* (Eight Bit, dir. Tetsuaki Watanabe) is unusually legible as a
study in this, because it is a sports series that spends most of its runtime
not animating football. Its recurring devices:

- **Still-frame push-ins on eyes.** The most common shot in the show. One
  drawing, hard push, held.
- **Speed lines over static bodies** instead of animated running.
- **Colour-flare background swaps** — the character is held, the background
  is replaced with a flat colour burst, which costs one fill.
- **Monologue over a single held illustration**, often for many seconds.
- **Manga-panel freeze frames**, complete with borders — the source medium
  used as an animation technique.
- **Extreme close-up fragmentation**: an eye, a boot, a ball, a mouth, cut at
  **4–8 frames per image**. A sequence of six stills reads as frantic action
  and costs six drawings.
- **3D for crowds and the ball**, so the expensive-to-animate mass is not
  hand-drawn at all.

The lesson is not "use stills". It is that the show is *loudest* exactly where
it is cheapest — the fragmentation cuts feel like the most animated moments in
the episode and contain the least animation.

## Failure modes

What makes reuse read as cheapness rather than economy:

- a static head on a looping body
- off-model drawings inside an otherwise consistent sequence
- a background that repeats before the shot ends
- the same mouth flap repeated on a different line
- two Ken-Burns pans back to back
- bank footage in the wrong tonal context
